\begin{abstract}
Hybrid classifiers placing a Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC) on a pretrained network are increasingly proposed for medical imaging, where labelled data are scarce. This study traces the source of such a head's advantage. On four MedMNIST datasets with 5 to 100 images per class, a four-qubit VQC head on frozen ResNet-18 features was compared with exactly parameter-matched classical heads and a linear model of the 81 sine and cosine functions the circuit computes. In prespecified tests, the VQC head outperformed the matched head at five images per class by 0.014 in area under the ROC curve (AUC; 95\% CI 0.003--0.026). Its lead shrank as data grew. A prespecified follow-up showed that the advantage depended on the projection trained with the head. With the projection fixed instead of trained, the VQC head fell behind at every size, significantly at five images per class. The VQC head never significantly outperformed the sine--cosine model, which led from ten images per class. Exploratory analyses showed the VQC's lead came mainly from one dataset and reversed at eight qubits. Removing the tanh input bound from both heads raised the lead at five images per class to 0.020. A small classical head beat a larger one at the smallest sizes, as did a re-uploading circuit against a 100-fold larger model of its function class. Both leads shrank or reversed as data grew. The VQC head's gain is consistent with regularisation by a small head trained with its projection. The results give no evidence of quantum advantage.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Quantum machine learning asks whether quantum computers can learn from data in ways that classical computers cannot, or can do so more efficiently~\cite{biamonte2017}. Noise in present quantum hardware limits the size of the circuits that can be run reliably~\cite{preskill2018}. Variational quantum algorithms, in which a classical optimiser trains the parameters of a quantum circuit, are a leading approach for working within this limit~\cite{cerezo2021vqa}. A circuit used as a classifier encodes the input into a quantum state, applies trainable gates and returns measured expectation values as its outputs~\cite{mitarai2018,schuld2020circuit}. With only a few qubits available, an image is too large to pass to the circuit directly. Mari \emph{et al.} addressed this with classical-to-quantum transfer learning~\cite{mari2020}. In their design, a pretrained network extracts image features, and a Variational Quantum Circuit (\vqc{}) placed between two trainable classical layers, together called a ``dressed'' quantum circuit, classifies them. Hybrid classifiers of this kind have since been applied to medical images, for example to COVID-19 detection in CT scans~\cite{acar2021} and to the MedMNIST benchmark~\cite{epa2026}.

In medical imaging, manual annotation is slow and expensive, so labelled data are scarce for many clinical tasks~\cite{litjens2017,cheplygina2019}. Networks pretrained on ImageNet~\cite{deng2009} have become the de facto starting point~\cite{raghu2019}. Theoretical results give small quantum models some appeal in this setting. Caro \emph{et al.} showed that the generalisation error of a quantum model with $T$ trainable gates, trained on $N$ examples, scales at worst as $\sqrt{T/N}$ up to a logarithmic factor~\cite{caro2022}. Abbas \emph{et al.} reported that some quantum neural networks train faster than comparable classical networks and reach a higher effective dimension, a capacity measure they linked to generalisation~\cite{abbas2021}.

However, a \vqc{} that encodes its inputs as rotation angles computes a partial Fourier series in those inputs, with frequencies fixed by the encoding gates~\cite{schuld2021}. Landman \emph{et al.} used this structure to approximate such circuits classically with random Fourier features, efficiently for several encoding strategies~\cite{landman2023}. Sweke \emph{et al.} derived the conditions under which this approximation is efficient for regression~\cite{sweke2025}. Schreiber \emph{et al.} built classical surrogates for a broad class of re-uploading models~\cite{perezsalinas2020} and found no quantum advantage in performance or trainability on the problems they studied. They concluded that generalisation is the only remaining candidate for an advantage~\cite{schreiber2023}. In a benchmark of 12 quantum models on 160 binary classification datasets, out-of-the-box classical models outperformed the quantum classifiers overall~\cite{bowles2024}. On brain-tumour MRI, Duong \emph{et al.} found a linear model of the trigonometric features that span a circuit head's function class more accurate than the circuit itself~\cite{duong2026}. On medical signals and images, Freinberger and Moser found classical networks significantly better than hybrid ones in most comparisons~\cite{freinberger2026}. Epa \emph{et al.} attributed a circuit head's robustness to label noise to its restricted frequency support~\cite{epa2026}. They identified a comparison with classical heads of matching smoothness as the direct extension of their work.

A fair comparison of heads must account for every difference between the arms other than the head. A matched parameter count does not ensure this on its own. Several recent studies match, or approximately match, the classical model's parameter count to the circuit's~\cite{martinperez2026,rubinos2026,epa2026}. Sammo \emph{et al.} found that much of the gap between a quantum head and its matched classical head came from an offset in the circuit's readout at initialisation. Calibrating that offset removed 61\% to 76\% of the gap~\cite{sammo2026}. In the dressed design, the trainable projection before the circuit can hold far more parameters than the circuit itself. In this study it holds 94\% to 97\% of them, depending on the number of classes. Arms with different parameterisations may also need different learning rates. Older models tuned as carefully as newer ones have overturned published rankings~\cite{melis2018}. Data sampling, initialisation and hyperparameter choice can markedly change the results of a benchmark~\cite{bouthillier2021}. In medical imaging, the error from a small test set can exceed the differences between methods~\cite{varoquaux2022}.

This paper asks where the gain comes from when a \vqc{} head outperforms a classical head with exactly the same number of parameters. The study uses frozen ResNet-18 features~\cite{he2016}, a trainable projection to four dimensions, one per qubit, and four MedMNIST datasets~\cite{yang2023} with 5 to 100 labelled images per class. Projections to eight and sixteen dimensions and training on the full datasets are reported as exploratory analyses.

The paper makes five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The \vqc{} head is compared with classical heads of exactly the same parameter count at three projection sizes. It is also compared with a classical linear model of the 81 sine and cosine functions that make up the circuit's output (the sine--cosine model). A restriction test compares classical heads of smaller and larger capacity, to test whether a more restricted head does better when data are scarce. A frozen-projection test replaces the trained projection with a fixed one (principal components or a random map). Exploratory checks train the \vqc{}, matched and Fourier heads without the input bound chosen for the circuit, each at a learning rate tuned for unbounded input, and run the learning-rate tuning for eleven configurations that had taken their rate from a tuned arm instead of being tuned themselves (Table~\ref{tab:lrcheck}). Table~\ref{tab:confounds} lists the sixteen confounds found and addressed during the study.
\item The main claims rest on twenty-three hypothesis tests written into an analysis plan before the experiments that used them~\cite{nosek2018} (Section~\ref{sec:prespec}) and corrected together for the false-discovery rate~\cite{benjamini1995}. Fourteen were supported, seven were not, one was opposite to its prediction and one showed a difference for which no direction had been predicted. A further nineteen exploratory checks had their predictions written before they ran. All are reported with their outcomes, including the eight whose predictions failed (Table~\ref{tab:checks}, Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory}).
\item At five labelled images per class the \vqc{} head outperformed the parameter-matched head by $+0.0142$ in area under the ROC curve (AUC; 95\% interval [$+0.0025$, $+0.0256$]). The difference fell with a slope of $-0.0046$ per doubling of the number of images ([$-0.0070$, $-0.0022$]). A prespecified follow-up showed that the lead depended on the projection being trained together with the head. The circuit never significantly outperformed the sine--cosine model of its own function class, which led from ten images per class. Exploratory analyses showed that the \vqc{}'s lead came mainly from BloodMNIST and reversed with eight qubits. Removing the input bound helped the circuit more than the matched head, once the circuit's learning rate was re-tuned for unbounded input. With both heads on unbounded input, the \vqc{}'s lead at five images per class was $+0.0198$ ([$+0.0104$, $+0.0307$]). In other exploratory comparisons, smaller learners led larger ones at the smallest sizes. This pattern is consistent with regularisation. The results give no evidence of quantum advantage.
\item Under simulated sensor noise, every small head lost far more F1 score than AUC. The \vqc{} head was no more robust than the others. In an exploratory analysis, its calibration error was the lowest of all heads, or within 0.005 of the lowest, at every noise level. This is consistent with a shifted decision threshold rather than poor calibration.
\item Each of the 35,740 reported training runs was re-run with the exact code version that produced it and reproduced its stored results bit for bit on the same type of device. Every run in which gradient clipping acted was repeated without it. The analysis plan, code, run records, trained weights and per-sample predictions are released (see Data and Code Availability).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data, the models and their function classes, the training protocol and the analysis plan. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the size of the gain and traces its source. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}, \ref{sec:limitations} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} give the discussion, the limitations and the conclusions. Appendix~\ref{app:tables} gives the learning rates and the provenance of every experiment, and Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory} reports further exploratory analyses.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Variational Quantum Classifiers and Their Function Class}
The circuits studied here descend from quantum circuit learning, in which the measured expectation values of a trainable circuit are the model output and gradients are obtained from the circuit itself~\cite{mitarai2018}. The strongly entangling layers used here follow the circuit-centric design of Schuld \emph{et al.}~\cite{schuld2020circuit}. Circuit templates differ considerably in expressibility and entangling capability~\cite{sim2019}. The gradients of random circuits also vanish exponentially as the number of qubits grows~\cite{mcclean2018}. An alternative template and the gradient variance are therefore examined as exploratory analyses (Appendix~\ref{sec:exploratory}).

What a circuit can compute depends on how its inputs are encoded. The Fourier result of Schuld, Sweke and Meyer~\cite{schuld2021} also covers repeated encoding, in which each repetition adds frequencies to the series. This data re-uploading makes even a single qubit a universal classifier~\cite{perezsalinas2020}. On a three-feature particle-physics task, Cass\'e \emph{et al.}~\cite{casse2024} found a single-qubit re-uploading circuit more accurate than a single-encoding circuit with the same number of parameters, and linked the gain to the added frequencies. Huang \emph{et al.}~\cite{huang2021} showed that classical learners with access to data can match quantum models on many tasks. Random Fourier features~\cite{landman2023,sweke2025} sample a circuit's spectrum because it can be too large to use in full. The four-qubit circuit used here spans only 81 functions, so the sine--cosine model (the Fourier head in the tables) uses all of them.

The capacity and generalisation results of Abbas \emph{et al.}~\cite{abbas2021} and Caro \emph{et al.}~\cite{caro2022} describe what quantum models could achieve in principle. They do not show whether a small circuit beats a classical head of the same size. Schuld and Killoran~\cite{schuld2022} questioned whether an advantage over classical models should remain the main aim of the field. In their benchmark, Bowles \emph{et al.}~\cite{bowles2024} found that removing entanglement often left a quantum model's performance as good or better. Hybrid models performed similarly to the classical models they were built on.

Berberich \emph{et al.}~\cite{berberich2024} showed that a quantum model's robustness to input noise depends on the norm of its data encoding, which training can adjust only when the encoding is trainable. Here the trainable projection in front of the circuit plays that role. A bounded circuit output therefore need not make the classifier robust (Section~\ref{sec:noise}). The exploratory hardware analysis (Appendix~\ref{sec:hardware}) uses the standard depolarising channel~\cite{nielsen2010}.

\subsection{Hybrid Quantum Transfer Learning}
Mari \emph{et al.}~\cite{mari2020} introduced the dressed circuit to show that quantum transfer learning is feasible. In their design, a linear layer compresses the 512 features of a pretrained ResNet-18 to the number of qubits. Senokosov \emph{et al.}~\cite{senokosov2024} built hybrid image classifiers from parallel quantum circuits and from a quanvolutional layer, and reported that the latter outperformed a classical model with the same number of weights. Mart\'{\i}n-P\'erez \emph{et al.}~\cite{martinperez2026} attached variational classifiers to frozen convolutional backbones on four binary image datasets, one of them brain-tumour MRI. They compared these with classical heads, including a parameter-matched multilayer perceptron, under ideal simulation, noisy emulation and on IBM hardware. The hybrid heads were competitive and in some settings more accurate, and noisy emulation lowered their accuracy. On tabular, digit and audio benchmarks, K\"olle \emph{et al.}~\cite{kolle2024} found that the classical layers drive most of the performance in hybrid transfer learning. They argued that this contribution is often attributed to the circuit. On PneumoniaMNIST, Tang, Nau and Maier~\cite{tang2025} found that a classical encoder trained end-to-end in front of a quantum convolutional network gave better and more consistent results across circuits than principal component analysis or an autoencoder. The hybrid did not reach a classical baseline's accuracy but used far fewer parameters.

Three recent studies ask questions close to this one. Freinberger and Moser~\cite{freinberger2026} found the classical models better in most comparisons. On their two image tasks, deeper classical front ends improved the hybrid models. Omitting a scaled $\tanh$ before angle encoding also improved the angle-encoded circuits. The present study bounds every head's input with $(\pi/2)\tanh$ (Eq.~(\ref{eq:squash})) and tests the bound's removal in exploratory analyses (Section~\ref{sec:squash}). Fulton, Tsapara and Fulton~\cite{fulton2026} compared real-valued, quaternion-valued and \vqc{} heads on identical frozen features from MNIST, FashionMNIST and CIFAR-10. The quaternion heads, classical models built on the same $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ geometry as single-qubit gates, outperformed shallow circuits on all three datasets. Entanglement reduced performance on pretrained ResNet-18 features. Duong \emph{et al.}~\cite{duong2026} placed a six-qubit circuit head on frozen and fine-tuned backbones for brain-tumour MRI under patient-grouped evaluation. With frozen features the circuit head was less accurate than classical layers of the same width. A linear model of the 729 trigonometric features spanning its function class was more accurate than the circuit.

In method, this study is closest to Sammo \emph{et al.}~\cite{sammo2026}, who matched parameter counts, and to Duong \emph{et al.}~\cite{duong2026}, who compared a circuit with a model of its own function class. Sammo \emph{et al.} compared a 32-parameter circuit head with a 31-parameter perceptron on paraphrase tasks. Besides the readout offset described in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, they found that an unweighted loss on imbalanced data collapsed the predictions to almost all one class, making the two heads look equivalent. Here, balanced training sets and, with full data, class weights prevent this collapse. Threshold-free AUC and a shared classifier with a bias term after every head guard against the readout offset.

These studies ask whether a hybrid head is competitive~\cite{freinberger2026,fulton2026,duong2026} or how such a comparison should be controlled~\cite{sammo2026}. The present study instead asks where a confirmed gain of the \vqc{} head comes from. It also tests a fixed projection in place of the trainable one, which holds 94\% to 97\% of the trainable parameters. The circuit is compared with classical heads of exactly equal parameter count and, as in the study of Duong \emph{et al.}, with classical models of its own function class.

Several studies posted while this work was in progress are also relevant. Epa \emph{et al.}~\cite{epa2026} found on six MedMNIST datasets that an eight-qubit circuit head behind a trainable bottleneck degraded less under label noise than a ReLU head of similar size. They attributed this to the restricted frequency support of single-encoding circuits. They proposed comparing classical heads with the same spectrum and testing whether re-uploading removes the effect. The sine--cosine models and re-uploading arms here do both, with scarce clean labels instead of noisy ones. Rubi\~nos Rodr\'{\i}guez \emph{et al.}~\cite{rubinos2026} replaced one dense layer of a convolutional network with an emulated four-qubit circuit and compared it with the parameter-matched original on retinal OCT and OASIS-1 brain MRI. The hybrid gained most at intermediate training-set sizes (about 800 to 2,000 retinal images). The original was most accurate on the largest sets. Yamaoka, Chan and Mitarai~\cite{yamaoka2026} tested whether the unitary structure of quantum classifiers acts as an implicit regulariser under limited supervision. They found its effectiveness to depend strongly on the geometry of the input features. Anthony \emph{et al.}~\cite{anthony2026} found that a 45-parameter quantum convolutional network learned a digit task on which an equally small classical network stayed at chance. On BreastMNIST it learned above chance with far fewer parameters but did not surpass an unconstrained classical network. Cruz \emph{et al.}~\cite{cruz2026} placed a ten-qubit circuit behind a $\tanh$-bounded linear projection of ResNet-50 features of blood-cell images, including the collection from which BloodMNIST is derived~\cite{acevedo2020}. They reported a higher macro-F1 than a classical model in which a 110-parameter layer took the place of the 40-parameter circuit. Outside medical imaging, Gillani \emph{et al.}~\cite{gillani2026} compared hybrid models for network-intrusion detection with classical models sharing their front end, and attributed the aggregate gains to classical preprocessing and regularisation.

\subsection{Data Scarcity and Evaluation in Medical Imaging}
ImageNet pretraining gave little benefit on two large medical imaging tasks~\cite{raghu2019}. Here the backbone is frozen and shared, so any such limitation affects all arms equally. Benchmark results vary with data sampling, initialisation and hyperparameters~\cite{bouthillier2021}. In medical imaging they also vary with the sampling error of small test sets~\cite{varoquaux2022}. The main comparisons therefore use 40 seeds and intervals that resample both seeds and test images (Section~\ref{sec:stats}). Rankings can change when baselines are tuned as carefully as newer models~\cite{melis2018}, so each main arm received its own tuned learning rate (Section~\ref{sec:lr}, Learning-Rate Selection). Metrics should answer the question asked~\cite{maierhein2024}. AUC, which measures ranking independently of any threshold, is therefore primary, with F1 and the expected calibration error reported alongside (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). The sensor-noise analysis adds Gaussian noise to the test images, following the corruption benchmark of Hendrycks and Dietterich~\cite{hendrycks2019} (Section~\ref{sec:noise}).

\subsection{Overview}
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} shows the full architecture pipeline, from input image to class scores. The arms of a comparison differ only in the head, each with its own initialisation and learning rate (Section~\ref{sec:lr}, Learning-Rate Selection). The two readout arms also have a wider classifier. The primary experiment trains five heads (the \vqc{}, matched, Fourier, re-upload \vqc{} and re-upload Fourier heads; Table~\ref{tab:arms}) on four datasets at five training sizes with 40 seeds. Its \vqc{} and matched arms form the primary comparison, which tests the two main hypotheses. Its seeds, learning rates, datasets and sizes define the primary protocol, which the follow-ups and some exploratory checks also use.

Every result carries one of three labels. Confirmatory results come from the 23 tests written into the analysis plan before their experiments ran. These tests share one false-discovery-rate correction. Prespecified follow-ups were added to the plan with a written prediction before they ran. One prespecified sensitivity analysis, with checkpoints selected on validation F1, repeats the confirmatory tests under a changed condition and adds no tests to the family. The confirmatory tests were also recomputed with clipping removed where it had acted, an exploratory check with predictions written in advance (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). All other results are exploratory (Section~\ref{sec:prespec}).

\subsection{Preprocessing and Feature Extraction}
The backbone is a ResNet-18~\cite{he2016} pretrained on ImageNet~\cite{deng2009} and cut after its third stage (\texttt{layer3}). Its output is average-pooled into a 256-dimensional feature vector $h$. The first three stages would shrink a $28\times28$ image to a $2\times2$ map, so each image was scaled to $[0,1]$ and upsampled bilinearly to $224\times224$. Greyscale images were copied to three channels, and all images were normalised with the ImageNet channel means and standard deviations.

The backbone was frozen except in the encoder-adaptation test and one compression check, which also trained \texttt{layer3} (Table~\ref{tab:lrused}). Frozen features were computed once, with batch normalisation in evaluation mode, and stored, so every arm trained on exactly the same features. An automated test and a separate verification run confirmed that no backbone weight or stored statistic changes during training. The test does detect such a change when batch normalisation is left in training mode. Augmentation would rule out stored features, so it was used only in the compression check.

\subsection{Projection and Input Bounding}
\label{sec:input}
A learned linear projection $z=Wh+b$, with $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times256}$, maps $h$ to $d$ numbers, one per qubit, adding 1,028 trainable parameters at $d=4$. The frozen-projection test and its follow-up replace it with a fixed projection, in one of two forms. The first projects the mean-centred features, without rescaling, onto their $d$ leading principal components. These are computed once per dataset from the whole training split without its labels (a fixed 10,002-image subsample for PathMNIST), since an $n$-shot subset can hold as few as ten images. Every arm and seed shares this projection. The second is a random matrix with entries from $\mathcal{N}(0,1/256)$ and zero bias, drawn anew for each run from its seed.

Every arm then receives the same bounded input,
\begin{equation}
\tilde z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tanh(z)\in\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)^{d}.
\label{eq:squash}
\end{equation}
The bound keeps each rotation angle within one period of the encoding, so that inputs far apart cannot give the same encoded state through its periodicity. The Fourier head uses the same periodic functions of its input, so whenever both heads receive the same input, bounded or not, its function class is exactly the circuit's. The matched, low-rank and identity heads have no use for the bound but receive it so that every head sees the same input. Section~\ref{sec:squash} reports the \vqc{}, matched and Fourier heads trained without it, each at a learning rate tuned for unbounded input.

\subsection{The Variational Quantum Head}
The \vqc{} head encodes $\tilde z$ with one $R_Y$ rotation per qubit and joins the $d$ single-qubit states by the tensor product $\bigotimes$,
\begin{equation}
|\psi(\tilde z)\rangle=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}R_Y(\tilde z_j)|0\rangle ,
\label{eq:encode}
\end{equation}
applies $L$ strongly entangling layers $U(\theta)$~\cite{schuld2020circuit}, and measures the Pauli-$X$ expectation on each qubit:
\begin{equation}
v_i(\tilde z)=\langle\psi(\tilde z)|\,U^{\dagger}(\theta)\,X_i\,U(\theta)\,|\psi(\tilde z)\rangle ,\qquad i=1,\dots,d .
\label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
Each strongly entangling layer applies a general three-angle rotation to every qubit, then a ring of CNOT gates that link neighbouring qubits in the first layer and qubits two positions apart in the second (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b). With $d=4$ and $L=2$ the circuit has $3Ld=24$ trainable angles, initialised from $\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^2)$. PennyLane's state-vector simulator~\cite{bergholm2018} computed the expectations exactly, without measurement sampling. The circuit was differentiated by backpropagation.

\subsection{The Function Class of the Quantum Head}
\label{sec:theory}
Each qubit's factor in \eqref{eq:encode} is $\cos(\tilde z_j/2)|0\rangle+\sin(\tilde z_j/2)|1\rangle$, so every amplitude $\psi_b$ of the encoded state is real. With $M(\theta)=U^\dagger(\theta)X_iU(\theta)$, the output is $v_i=\sum_{b,b'}\psi_b\psi_{b'}M_{bb'}$. Because $\psi$ is real, only the real symmetric part of the Hermitian matrix $M(\theta)$ contributes. Each product $\psi_b\psi_{b'}$ factorises over the qubits into terms $\cos^2(\tilde z_j/2)=(1+\cos\tilde z_j)/2$, $\sin^2(\tilde z_j/2)=(1-\cos\tilde z_j)/2$ or $\sin(\tilde z_j/2)\cos(\tilde z_j/2)=(\sin\tilde z_j)/2$. Each output is therefore a linear combination of products of $1$, $\cos\tilde z_j$ and $\sin\tilde z_j$:
\begin{equation}
v_i(\tilde z)=\sum_{\alpha\in\{0,\cos,\sin\}^d} a_\alpha(\theta)\prod_{j=1}^{d}f_{\alpha_j}(\tilde z_j),
\label{eq:span}
\end{equation}
with $f_0=1$, $f_{\cos}=\cos$ and $f_{\sin}=\sin$. This span of $3^d$ fixed functions, 81 at $d=4$, corresponds to the frequency spectrum $\{-1,0,1\}^d$ that the general result of Schuld \emph{et al.}~\cite{schuld2021} predicts for this encoding. Each output's coefficient vector $a(\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{81}$ depends smoothly on only 24 parameters, so the reachable vectors form a set of dimension at most 24. The \vqc{} is therefore a particular, restricted way of choosing coefficients in a classical 81-dimensional function space.

Two variants serve as controls. Encoding each input twice (re-uploading) gives the spectrum $\{-2,\dots,2\}^d$, so each output lies in a span of $5^d$ functions (625 at $d=4$). The derivation applies unchanged to the six two-qubit correlators $\langle X_iX_j\rangle$, so measuring them as well gives the classifier ten functions from the same $3^d$-function span instead of four.

Equation~\eqref{eq:span} was checked numerically in two independent ways. Least-squares fits of the $3^d$ functions to PennyLane's outputs for six $(d,L)$ configurations left errors below $4\times10^{-15}$. A basis with the wrong frequencies left 0.91, which shows that the test can fail. A plain NumPy implementation, sharing no code with the first, matched the $3^d$ functions to the single-encoding circuit and the two-qubit readout, and the $5^d$ functions to the re-uploading circuit, all with errors below $3.5\times10^{-15}$. The $3^d$ functions alone left an error of 0.27 on the re-uploading circuit. Fig.~\ref{fig:function_class} shows a trained head from a reported run next to its 81-term classical expansion.

The matched head has $d^2+2d$ parameters ($A$, $a$ and $s$), equal to the circuit's $3Ld=6d$ at $L=2$ only when $d=4$. The low-rank head has $2dk+2d$, equal to $6d$ at $k=2$ for every $d$, so with $k=2$ it is the matched control in the exploratory runs at $d=8$ and $d=16$. Varying $k$ changes its capacity without limiting the rank of its map, because $I+UV^{\!\top}$ is invertible for almost every $U$ and $V$. With $k=0$ only the scale and bias remain (8 parameters). With $k=8>d$, the matrix $I+UV^{\!\top}$ can be any $d\times d$ matrix (72 parameters). The restriction test compares these two extremes to ask whether limited capacity, acting as a regulariser, explains the gain.

At $d=4$ the Fourier head is matched on the \emph{function class}, not on parameter count. It is a linear model, plus bias, of the 80 functions $\cos(\omega^{\top}\tilde z)$ and $\sin(\omega^{\top}\tilde z)$ of Table~\ref{tab:arms}. Expanding the products in \eqref{eq:span} gives sines and cosines of this form, so with the bias these functions span the same 81-dimensional space. One $\omega$ per pair $\pm\omega$ suffices, since $-\omega$ gives the same two functions up to sign. The re-upload Fourier head does the same for the re-upload \vqc{}, whose 625-function class is spanned by its 624 functions and the bias. These heads ask whether the circuit adds anything to a classical model trained directly on the functions it can represent.

The other circuit arms each change one design choice of the \vqc{}. The two-qubit readout passes ten functions from the same span to a wider classifier. The padded readout has the same width but only repeats the four single-qubit outputs, so comparing the two isolates the effect of the extra measurements. The alternative ansatz, examined in an exploratory analysis, replaces the strongly entangling layers with basic ones at the same 24 parameters. PennyLane sets the CNOT range of each strongly entangling layer by its position within a call. The re-upload \vqc{} applies one layer per encoding block, so it also differs from the \vqc{} in the wiring of its second layer. The range-1 \vqc{}, added in an exploratory check (Section~\ref{sec:fourier}), has this wiring with a single encoding and so separates the two differences. The identity head passes $\tilde z$ straight to the classifier.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
Table~\ref{tab:training} lists the training settings, which differ between arms only in the learning rate and the initialisation of each head type. Weight decay applies to the circuit angles as well as to classical weights. Class weighting has no effect on the balanced $n$-shot training sets and matters only with full data.

\emph{Early stopping and checkpoint selection.} The checkpoint kept is the first epoch with the highest validation AUC. Training stops after 30 epochs without improvement, or at 100 epochs, but not before $\min\!\big(\max(20,\lfloor 200/B\rfloor),\,50\big)$ epochs, where $B$ is the number of training batches per epoch. This minimum is 50 epochs at $n\le10$, where an epoch is only one or two optimisation steps. Selection on validation macro-F1 is a prespecified sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:primary}).

\emph{Gradient clipping.} Gradients are clipped to a global norm of 20, a value fixed before any reported run and set high so that clipping would rarely act. A threshold that acts more often for some arms trains them differently. The norm of every step was therefore recorded (Section~\ref{sec:audit}), and every result was recomputed without clipping wherever it acted (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\emph{Optimisation budget.} The 100-epoch cap was reached in 57\% to 63\% of the primary runs, depending on the arm, and in 71 of the 80 full-data runs. An exploratory check repeated part of the primary experiment and the full-data runs of the two primary arms with ten times the budget (1,000 epochs, patience 200). Each long run matched its 100-epoch counterpart bit for bit up to the epoch at which that run had stopped. For the full-data runs, the counterpart was the GPU re-execution. The two budgets therefore differ only in how long training continues (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Learning-Rate Selection}
\label{sec:lr}
Circuit angles are periodic and linear weights are not, so heads can have different best learning rates. A single shared rate could favour one arm. A weak result for the \vqc{} could then not be told apart from a poorly suited rate. Each of the four main arms was given the rate with the highest mean validation AUC among $3\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$, $3\times10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}$, at $d=4$ on all four datasets at $n\in\{5,20,100\}$ (the smallest, middle and largest of the five training sizes, fixed in the tuning script before any tuning run), with five tuning seeds disjoint from the confirmatory seeds. The same rates were used at $d=8$ and $d=16$. Each arm received one rate for all of its parameters, datasets and sizes. At $n=5$ the validation set has only ten images per class, and choosing among rates on so few would mostly reward chance. Every arm's best rate was the largest candidate, $10^{-2}$. Before any confirmatory run, the grid was extended to $3\times10^{-2}$ and $10^{-1}$ with the same seeds and criterion. Every best rate then lay inside the grid, and only the identity head's rate changed (Table~\ref{tab:lr}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). The primary comparison and the function-class test therefore do not depend on the extension.

Arms added after tuning took the rate of the tuned arm they are built from. The re-upload, range-1, readout and alternative-ansatz circuits took the \vqc{}'s rate, the low-rank head the matched head's and the re-upload Fourier head the Fourier head's (Table~\ref{tab:lrused}). The original restriction, frozen-projection and hardware-feasibility runs used the untuned rate of $10^{-3}$ and were later repeated at the tuned rate (Section~\ref{sec:prespec} and Appendix~\ref{sec:hardware}).

Two later checks ran the same procedure (Table~\ref{tab:lrcheck}). The first, with predictions written in advance, covered the eleven configurations that had inherited a rate. It confirmed the inherited rate for eight, including every circuit arm, and selected $3\times10^{-3}$ for three classical heads at $d=4$. The low-rank head with $k=2$ appears at $d=4$ only in exploratory comparisons, and its gap is within the 0.005 that the plan allowed. The comparisons involving the other two were repeated at the selected rates (Section~\ref{sec:restriction}). The second, without a prediction, tuned the \vqc{}, the matched head and the Fourier head for unbounded input. It selected $3\times10^{-2}$ for the \vqc{} and $10^{-2}$ for the other two (Section~\ref{sec:squash}).

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:prespec}
Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses} lists the hypothesis tests. The primary statistic is the difference in test AUC between the \vqc{} and matched heads,
\begin{equation}
\Delta(n)=\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{VQC}}(n)-\mathrm{AUC}_{\mathrm{matched}}(n),
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
averaged over the four datasets with equal weight. The two primary tests predict $\Delta(5)>0$ and a negative slope of $\Delta$ on $\log_2 n$. The plan also fixed in advance what would be claimed for each outcome of these two tests. Both held. Under that rule, this licensed a claim of a scarcity-dependent advantage of the \vqc{} head, attributed to restriction of the function class. The confirmatory and prespecified follow-up tests of restriction did not support that attribution. Later exploratory comparisons are consistent with it but do not establish it (Section~\ref{sec:restriction}). The paper therefore claims less than the rule allowed. The scarcity effect itself was first seen in earlier diagnostic runs with the rank-limited control and F1-based checkpoint selection. No number from those runs is reported. The restriction explanation was proposed after them and was given its own test.

The original restriction and frozen-projection tests used the untuned rate of $10^{-3}$ and only 10 and 5 seeds, so a null result could not distinguish no effect from too little power. Both were repeated under the primary protocol as follow-ups, with predictions written before they ran. The follow-ups are corrected separately and change no confirmatory verdict. For the original frozen-projection test, $\Delta(5)$ was chosen over the slope as the test statistic after the slopes had been seen, and the slopes were null as well. Nineteen further checks also had their predictions written before they ran. They are exploratory, because some were declared so in the plan and the others were prompted by earlier results or added after the confirmatory analysis. Table~\ref{tab:checks} in Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory} gives the purpose, prediction and outcome of each. Every experiment first ran on a commit whose copy of the plan already contained its hypotheses (Table~\ref{tab:provenance}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). The released run records allow a reader to check this (see Data and Code Availability).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
AUC, the primary metric, is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of the positive-class probability for binary tasks~\cite{fawcett2006}, and the macro-average of the one-versus-rest AUCs for multi-class tasks. It depends only on how the model ranks the test images, not on a decision threshold. Macro-F1, the unweighted mean of the per-class F1 scores with each image assigned to its most probable class, depends on the decisions the model makes. The expected calibration error (ECE)~\cite{guo2017} averages the gap between confidence and accuracy over 15 equal-width bins of the top predicted probability, weighted by bin size.

The noise analysis uses the F1--AUC gap
\begin{equation}
G(\sigma)=\frac{F_1(0)-F_1(\sigma)}{F_1(0)}-\frac{\mathrm{AUC}(0)-\mathrm{AUC}(\sigma)}{\mathrm{AUC}(0)} ,
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
the relative loss in macro-F1 minus the relative loss in AUC at noise level $\sigma$. A positive $G$ means that noise harms the model's decisions proportionally more than its ranking. (The plan first named the ratio of the two losses, which is undefined when the AUC loss is near zero, so the difference was fixed before the data existed.) The encoder-adaptation test uses
\begin{equation}
I=\frac{1}{20}\sum_{c}\Big(\big|\Delta_c^{\mathrm{frozen}}\big|-\big|\Delta_c^{\mathrm{adaptive}}\big|\Big),
\label{eq:enc}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_c$ is the \vqc{}--matched difference in AUC in dataset--size cell $c$, with the backbone frozen or with its third stage trainable. A positive $I$ means that the choice of head matters less when the backbone adapts.

\subsection{Statistical Inference}
\label{sec:stats}
All intervals and $p$-values come from a nested paired bootstrap with $B=2000$ replicates. Within each dataset--size cell, each replicate resamples the test images and the seeds with replacement, identically for both arms of a contrast. Pooled statistics, which weight the datasets equally, and least-squares slopes on $\log_2 n$ are computed within each replicate. Intervals are 95\% percentile intervals. Two-sided $p$-values use the $+1$ correction~\cite{davison1997}, $p=2\min\{(k_{\le0}+1)/(B+1),\,(k_{\ge0}+1)/(B+1)\}$, where $k_{\le0}$ and $k_{\ge0}$ count replicates on either side of zero. The smallest attainable unadjusted value is $2/2001\approx0.001$, reached when every bootstrap replicate lies on one side of zero. In the confirmatory family, an adjusted value of 0.002 implies such a raw value. In the smaller families it can also arise from a single replicate on the other side. The datasets are treated as fixed, so the intervals support inference about these four datasets. Leaving one dataset out (Section~\ref{sec:scope}) shows how far the result generalises. Resampling the test images matters here. On BreastMNIST, with 156 test images, the standard error of a single AUC near the observed 0.67 is at least 0.04 by the Hanley--McNeil approximation~\cite{hanley1982}, larger than the primary effect (0.014) and than every pooled difference between heads in this study. A test over seeds alone would ignore this error~\cite{bouthillier2021}.

The 23 confirmatory tests were corrected once with the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure~\cite{benjamini1995} at a false-discovery rate of 0.05. A test is \emph{supported} if $p_{\mathrm{adj}}\le0.05$ and its estimate has the predicted sign. A significant estimate of the opposite sign is reported as \emph{opposite to prediction}, and one where no direction was predicted as a \emph{difference without prediction}. AUC differences below 0.01, smaller than the test-set sampling error on the smallest dataset, were declared negligible in advance. Effect sizes are paired $d_z$, the mean of the per-seed paired differences divided by their standard deviation, pooled over cells as each test pools. Each follow-up has its own correction, and exploratory analyses are corrected within each analysis.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:audit}
Every run is stored with its configuration, git commit and per-sample test predictions. Recomputing the test AUC from the stored predictions reproduced the recorded value for every run of the primary experiment, to within $2.2\times10^{-16}$. An automated suite of 66 tests checks backbone freezing, the data pipeline, the identity \eqref{eq:span} and parameter parity. Parity requires 24 parameters for the \vqc{}, matched and low-rank ($k=2$) heads at $d=4$, and 48 and 96 for the low-rank head at $d=8$ and $d=16$.

After the analyses were complete, every reported run was executed again with the code of the commit that had recorded it and compared with its record in every stored number and per-sample probability. On the same type of device as the original, all 35,740 reported runs reproduced exactly (20,220 on GPU and 15,520 on CPU). Each CPU comparison pairs runs on one processor type. Runs that had built the feature cache were reproduced by rebuilding it, since building it changes the random state before initialisation.

Across devices, runs do not reproduce exactly, because small floating-point differences can grow during training, most of all in the circuit head. Across 800 \vqc{} runs at $n=5$ to 100 trained on CPU instead of GPU, individual runs differed by 0.016 AUC on average (up to 0.22, with 100 of 800 identical). The mean over all 800 moved by 0.0004, and per-cell means over 40 seeds by at most 0.007. With full data, the \vqc{}'s mean AUC moved by 0.021 on BloodMNIST and 0.007 on PathMNIST, and every classical head's by at most 0.0007. Every comparison between arms therefore pairs arms trained on the same type of device.

The re-execution also recorded the gradient norm of every step. In the experiments of the original plan, clipping at 20 acted in about one run in ten, mostly in classical heads. Two-thirds of these runs used a Fourier head. Every such run was repeated without clipping. For the full-data runs this was done on their GPU re-executions, and for the six in which clipping acted on CPU, also on CPU. In the checks of Table~\ref{tab:checks}, clipping acted in 2,480 runs, most of them in Fourier heads. Each was repeated without clipping on the same processor type and matched its clipped run bit for bit, in every gradient norm, up to and including the first step at which clipping acted (805,769 steps in all). One session assigned a different processor diverged from the first step and was repeated on the original type. Each analysis was recomputed with the unclipped runs in their place (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Hardware and Software}
Most runs used one 20\,GB partition (MIG 3g.20gb) of an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB GPU. The full-data runs ran on CPU. So did the checks that needed new training runs after the confirmatory analysis (AMD EPYC 7B12), namely the circuit-wiring and input-bound checks and the learning-rate checks with the comparisons re-run at selected rates. The original re-upload function-class control is an exception. Its arms are those of the primary experiment, and it ran on GPU. The software was Python~3.10, PyTorch~2.4.1~\cite{paszke2019}, PennyLane~0.42.3~\cite{bergholm2018}, NumPy~1.26.4 and medmnist~3.0.2, with exact versions pinned in the released lock file.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Clipping and to the Training Budget}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
\emph{Clipping (exploratory).} Removing clipping where it had acted changed no confirmatory verdict (Table~\ref{tab:family}, last column). Twenty-two of the 23 estimates moved by at most 0.0002. The exception was encoder adaptation, where clipping had acted in 101 of 800 runs, most with the backbone's third stage trainable. Its statistic moved from $-0.0048$ ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.12$) to $-0.0089$ ($[-0.0217,-0.0006]$, $p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.055$). It remained not supported, with the sign opposite to the prediction. Learning-rate selection was unchanged for every arm. In the checks of Table~\ref{tab:checks}, removing clipping moved no input-bound estimate by more than 0.0002 and changed no learning-rate selection. It did change the comparison of the re-upload circuit with its 2,500-parameter fit, where clipping had acted most (Section~\ref{sec:restriction}).

\emph{Training budget (exploratory).} With a cap of 1,000 epochs instead of 100, on the same 10 seeds, $\Delta(5)$ was $+0.0305$ ($[+0.0079,+0.0572]$) against $+0.0314$ at 100 epochs. The slope stayed negative ($-0.0068$, $[-0.0119,-0.0024]$, against $-0.0079$). At $n=100$, $\Delta$ moved from $-0.0086$ ([$-0.0148$, $-0.0027$]) to $-0.0040$ ([$-0.0094$, $+0.0013$]). Pooled AUC changed by less than 0.01 for every arm at every $n$, except for the 2,500-parameter Fourier head ($+0.0174$ at $n=10$, $+0.0102$ at $n=50$), for which the 100-epoch cap was binding. Every contrast whose 100-epoch interval excluded zero kept its sign. Section~\ref{sec:scope} gives the full-data result under both budgets.

\subsection{Scope of the Effect (Exploratory)}
\label{sec:scope}
The confirmatory test pools four datasets at one bottleneck size. These analyses, declared exploratory in the plan, examine how far it extends.

\emph{Across datasets.} At $n=5$ the advantage came from BloodMNIST ($+0.0446$, $[+0.0117,+0.0770]$; Fig.~\ref{fig:scope}), the only dataset whose interval excluded zero. The others were $+0.0104$ (BreastMNIST), $+0.0056$ (PneumoniaMNIST) and $-0.0038$ (PathMNIST), all with intervals spanning zero. Leaving BloodMNIST out reduced the pooled $\Delta(5)$ to $+0.0041$ ($[-0.0071,+0.0155]$). Leaving out any other dataset kept it positive with an interval excluding zero ($+0.0155$, $+0.0171$ and $+0.0202$ without BreastMNIST, PneumoniaMNIST and PathMNIST). PathMNIST favoured the matched head at $n=20$, 50 and 100 (about $-0.02$ each, intervals excluding zero), and BloodMNIST did at $n=50$ and 100. Without clipping, one per-dataset estimate moved by 0.0004 and no interval changed its side of zero. The four-dimensional bottleneck is itself a material constraint on BloodMNIST. Against a 256-dimensional projection, compression cost the identity head 0.018 to 0.055 AUC on that dataset at every size. No cost was detected on the two binary datasets (Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory}).

\subsection{The Circuit's Function Class, Re-uploading and Readout}
\label{sec:fourier}
The circuit never significantly outperformed a classical model of its own function class. The Fourier head, a direct linear fit over the 81 functions that make up the circuit's output, outperformed the \vqc{} by 0.016 to 0.021 AUC at every $n\ge10$, with all four tests supported. At $n=5$ the difference was $-0.0095$ and not significant (Table~\ref{tab:family}). With 324 parameters against the circuit's 24, the Fourier head tests whether the circuit's restricted way of choosing coefficients adds anything to an unrestricted learner over the same functions. At $n\ge10$ it does not.

The same pattern held on CPU and on unbounded input (exploratory; Table~\ref{tab:squash}, Fig.~\ref{fig:fourier}). The Fourier head led from $n=10$ with the bound, and from $n=20$ with both heads unbounded, each at its own tuned rate. No difference was detected at the smaller sizes.

Shaded: 95\% nested bootstrap intervals. The re-uploading circuit's comparison with its own function class is in Fig.~\ref{fig:restriction}b.\label{fig:fourier}}

Re-uploading the input helped at every $n\ge10$, by about 0.01 AUC. The plan had predicted harm at $n\in\{5,10\}$ and benefit at $n\in\{50,100\}$. The benefit at 50 and 100 was supported. At $n=10$ the result was opposite to the prediction. At $+0.0090$ it is statistically significant but below the negligibility threshold. The two circuits also differ in the wiring of their second entangling layer. An exploratory check therefore trained all three circuits side by side on the same CPU for each of the 40 seeds. Changing only the wiring moved AUC by $+0.001$ to $+0.003$, with no interval excluding zero, although at $n=5$ the interval reaches $+0.010$. Adding only the second encoding raised AUC by $+0.007$ to $+0.009$ from $n=10$ to 100, every interval above zero, with no effect detected at $n=5$. Both predictions held, so the re-uploading difference comes mainly from the second encoding. Section~\ref{sec:restriction} compares the re-uploading circuit with a fit over its own function class.

Measuring the six two-qubit correlators $\langle X_iX_j\rangle$ as well as the four single-qubit expectations, from the same circuit with the same 24 parameters, improved AUC by $+0.0114$ over a padded readout of the same width and by $+0.0111$ over the single-qubit readout. Both tests were supported. The correlators lie in the same $3^d$ span (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), so this does not escape classical simulability. It indicates that four single-qubit measurements reach less of the span than the circuit can represent.

\subsection{The Input Bound (Exploratory)}
\label{sec:squash}
The bound of \eqref{eq:squash} was chosen for the circuit's rotation angles, and the matched head has no use for it. In a 10-seed ablation that removed it from the classical heads only (Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory}), all four of the matched head's estimates at $n=5$ were positive, by amounts similar to the primary advantage. Three checks under the primary protocol followed, each with predictions written before it ran (Table~\ref{tab:checks}). The first trained the matched head with and without the bound at its tuned rate, $10^{-2}$. The second trained the \vqc{} and the matched head on unbounded input, each at the rate selected for that input ($3\times10^{-2}$ and $10^{-2}$; Section~\ref{sec:lr}, Learning-Rate Selection). The third did the same for the Fourier head ($10^{-2}$). The bound has no parameters, so for a given seed both variants of a head start from the same weights and see the same batches. Every arm was trained on CPU with the 40 confirmatory seeds and all 20 cells. The bounded \vqc{} is that of the wiring check.

Removing the bound raised the matched head's AUC by $+0.0153$ at $n=5$ (Table~\ref{tab:squash}, Fig.~\ref{fig:squash}). With its rate re-tuned for unbounded input, the \vqc{} gained 0.0130 to 0.0197 at every size. The Fourier head did not change detectably. As the first check predicted, the bounded \vqc{}'s lead over the unbounded matched head at $n=5$ was then only $+0.0002$, with the \vqc{} behind from $n=20$. That comparison removed the bound from one arm only. With both heads unbounded, each at its own tuned rate, the \vqc{}'s lead was $+0.0198$ ([$+0.0104$, $+0.0307$], $p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.005$) at $n=5$ and $+0.0093$ at $n=10$. It was not detected from $n=20$ and fell as data grew (slope $-0.0040$ per doubling, [$-0.0061$, $-0.0020$], descriptive). The second check's prediction therefore failed. Against the Fourier head, the unbounded circuit still showed no significant lead (Section~\ref{sec:fourier}).

\subsection{Restriction and Model Size}
\label{sec:restriction}
The restriction test examines the proposed mechanism with classical heads only. It compares the most and least restricted low-rank heads, $k=0$ (8 parameters) and $k=8$ (72 parameters), through $\Delta_0(n)=\mathrm{AUC}_{k=0}-\mathrm{AUC}_{k=8}$. If restriction regularises, the smaller head should help at $n=5$ and help less as $n$ grows. Neither confirmatory test was supported (Table~\ref{tab:family}). That run used 10 seeds at the untuned rate, and its interval for $\Delta_0(5)$, [$-0.0380$, $+0.0114$], could not exclude a positive effect of up to 0.011. The prespecified follow-up under the primary protocol, with both heads at the inherited rate $10^{-2}$, predicted no restriction effect. It gave $\Delta_0(5)=+0.0097$ ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.074$) and a slope of $-0.0016$ ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.143$; Table~\ref{tab:followups}). Neither survives correction, so the prediction of no restriction effect stands formally. The smaller head was nevertheless ahead at every $n$.

The check of inherited rates (Section~\ref{sec:lr}) then found $3\times10^{-3}$ better than $10^{-2}$ for the $k=8$ head, while $10^{-2}$ remained best for the $k=0$ head. With each head at its selected rate, a check with its prediction written in advance gave $\Delta_0(5)=+0.0131$ ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.040$, corrected within the analysis) and $+0.0074$ at $n=10$. At the selected rates, the smaller head therefore led at the smallest sizes and fell behind as data grew. This is the scarcity-dependent pattern that restriction predicts. It appeared only with the larger head at its selected rate, which was chosen by mean validation AUC pooled over $n\in\{5,20,100\}$. Compared with $10^{-2}$, that rate lowered the larger head's AUC at $n=5$ by about 0.003 and raised it by 0.004 to 0.009 at every larger size. The result is exploratory, depends on the learning rate of the larger head, and concerns two classical heads, not the circuit.

The re-uploading circuit and a direct fit over its 625-function span give a second comparison of a small and a large learner over one function class, with 24 against 2,500 parameters (Table~\ref{tab:reupfc} in Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory}). Its prediction, written in advance, was that the circuit would score lower at $n\ge10$. It failed (Fig.~\ref{fig:restriction}b). At the inherited rate the circuit led by $+0.0150$ and $+0.0148$ at $n=5$ and 10 and was behind at $n=100$. Clipping had acted in 677 of the fit's 800 runs, and without it the circuit's lead at $n=5$ was $+0.0278$. With the fit at its selected rate, $3\times10^{-3}$, the lead at $n=5$ was $+0.0159$ as recorded and $+0.0151$ without clipping. A further prediction, of a smaller lead than at the inherited rate, therefore failed as recorded and held without clipping. In all six 40-seed versions (GPU and CPU, with and without clipping, at the inherited and the selected rate), the circuit led at $n=5$ and 10 with intervals above zero and was behind at $n=100$. In a 10-seed run with 1,000 epochs, the lead at $n=10$ fell from $+0.0328$ to $+0.0204$, and at $n=50$ the circuit was behind ($-0.0106$, interval excluding zero).

Both comparisons show a smaller learner ahead at the smallest sizes, with a lead that shrinks or reverses as data grow. This is the pattern expected if a restricted model regularises where data are scarce. The single-encoding circuit against its 324-parameter fit, a ratio of about 13 against 100, showed no significant lead at $n=5$ in any version (Section~\ref{sec:fourier}). These analyses are exploratory. They do not show that restriction produces the \vqc{}'s lead over the matched head. That head has the same number of parameters, and the lead disappears when the projection is fixed (Section~\ref{sec:projection}).

\subsection{Sensor Noise}
\label{sec:noise}
Gaussian noise with $\sigma\in\{0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20\}$ was added to the test images at their native $28\times28$ resolution and channel count and clipped to $[0,1]$. The images then passed through the same pipeline. Heads were trained on clean images, and every arm saw identical corrupted images for a given seed and noise level. Seven heads were evaluated over the four datasets, five training sizes and 10 seeds.

The noise-gap test predicted that the \vqc{}'s macro-F1 would degrade more than its AUC, and it did ($G=0.356$ to 0.401, supported at every $\sigma$; Table~\ref{tab:family}). The plan had read such a pattern as a calibration failure of the circuit. The controls do not support that reading (Table~\ref{tab:noise}, Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}). All seven heads showed the pattern, with $G$ between 0.297 and 0.381 for the other six. The \vqc{} had the largest $G$ at every $\sigma$, but its excess over the matched head was modest ($+0.053$, $+0.065$, $+0.044$ and $+0.048$, paired, all intervals excluding zero; exploratory). Its expected calibration error was the lowest of the seven heads, or within 0.005 of the lowest, at every $\sigma$. The Fourier and identity heads were the worst calibrated. The pattern is consistent with noise shifting the scores relative to a decision threshold learned on clean images. Such a shift hurts F1 while leaving the ranking, and so AUC, nearly intact. Every head tested showed it, so it is not a failure specific to the circuit.

The shared classifier can rescale any head's output, so a bounded output does not by itself make a head robust. Sensitivity was therefore measured as the largest finite-difference ratio over random inputs and directions, divided by the head's largest absolute output (exploratory; Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory}). As predicted before any trained weights existed, the trained \vqc{}, rebuilt from the re-executed runs, was more sensitive than the matched head. It was more sensitive in all 200 paired runs on uniform inputs (geometric mean ratio 2.74) and in 174 of 200 on each run's test latents (ratio 1.82). It was also more sensitive than the low-rank head at $d=8$ and 16 (ratios 2.03 to 2.58). A concurrent study reports trained circuit heads as two to four times \emph{less} sensitive than a ReLU control~\cite{epa2026}, with a measure not normalised by output range. Here a bounded output did not make the circuit head more robust. This is consistent with the view that robustness depends on the encoding and the weights, not on boundedness alone~\cite{berberich2024}.

\subsection{Summary of the Exploratory Checks}
\label{sec:checks}
Table~\ref{tab:checks} in Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory} lists the nineteen exploratory checks whose predictions were written before they ran, with the purpose, prediction and outcome of each. The predictions held in eight, failed in eight and held only in part in three. Several of the failures changed the reading of the results. With both heads unbounded, the circuit's lead was larger, not absent, and the restriction and re-uploading comparisons moved towards a regularisation reading. None of these checks changes a confirmatory verdict.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{What the advantage is.} The confirmatory lead of the \vqc{} head at five images per class survived re-execution. In exploratory checks it also survived the removal of clipping, a tenfold training budget and the removal of the input bound from both heads. Without the bound it was larger. The controls narrow it in three ways. First, it is small, came mainly from one dataset, reversed at $d=8$, and was not detected at $d=16$ or with full data. Second, it appeared only when the projection was trained together with the head. Third, the circuit never significantly outperformed a classical linear model of its own function class, so nothing here is evidence of a quantum advantage.

\emph{Regularisation.} Two exploratory comparisons set a smaller learner against a larger one. One compared two classical low-rank heads at their selected rates. The other compared the re-uploading circuit with a fit of its function class with about 100 times as many parameters. In both, the smaller learner led at the smallest sizes, and its lead shrank or reversed as data grew. This is the pattern expected of regularisation by a restricted model, and it has the shape of the \vqc{}'s lead over the matched head. It does not show that restriction produces that lead. The two heads have the same number of parameters, and the lead disappears when the projection is fixed. The projection also trains differently behind the circuit, with about one fewer of the four latent coordinates active at $n=5$. A small, bounded head trained jointly with a 1,028-parameter projection may regularise the projection as well as itself. The data are consistent with this account but cannot separate it from other properties of the circuit head, such as the gradients it returns to the projection.

\emph{Shared preprocessing.} Applying one input transformation to every arm is a natural way to keep a comparison fair, but a transformation chosen for one arm can handicap the others. Here the bound was chosen for the circuit. Removing it from the classical arm alone made the circuit's lead undetectable, which suggested an artefact of the bound. Removing it from both arms, each at a rate tuned for unbounded input, showed instead that the bound, together with the rate it permits, had handicapped the circuit more. A one-sided ablation of shared preprocessing can therefore mislead in either direction.

\emph{Relation to prior work.} The results agree with a large benchmark in which out-of-the-box classical models performed better overall~\cite{bowles2024}. They also agree with recent hybrid studies in which classical heads were mostly better~\cite{freinberger2026,fulton2026,duong2026}, and with dequantisation results that place these circuits inside classical function classes~\cite{schuld2021,landman2023,sweke2025,schreiber2023}. As in the study of Duong \emph{et al.}~\cite{duong2026}, a linear model of the circuit's own trigonometric features was at least as accurate as the circuit. As Freinberger and Moser found for angle-encoded circuits~\cite{freinberger2026}, removing the scaled $\tanh$ improved this circuit. That comparison was exploratory and descriptive, at $d=4$, with each variant at its own tuned rate. The results add two points. First, a positive result can hold under exact parameter matching and still not belong to the circuit. Here it depended on the projection being trained together with the head. This is consistent with analyses that attribute much of a hybrid model's performance to its classical parts~\cite{kolle2024} or to preprocessing and regularisation~\cite{gillani2026}. Second, the circuit's apparent fragility under noise was shared by every head tested. These controls bear on the restricted-spectrum explanation offered for a circuit head's robustness to label noise~\cite{epa2026}, although with scarce clean labels instead of noisy ones. Here a classical fit over the circuit's spectrum was at least as accurate as the circuit, and the advantage of small models appeared among classical heads too. Capacity and generalisation arguments for quantum models~\cite{abbas2021,caro2022} are not contradicted, but they are not required here. A classical model of the circuit's own function class did at least as well as the circuit. These findings concern one hybrid design under strong controls and do not show that no circuit head can help in medical imaging.

\emph{Implications for evaluating hybrid classifiers.} Each of the following controls changed or bounded a conclusion in this study:
\begin{itemize}
\item a classical head matched exactly in parameter count and checked to be full rank;
\item a direct fit over the circuit's own function class;
\item a frozen-projection control for the trainable layers outside the head;
\item each arm also trained without input transformations chosen for another arm, at a rate tuned for that input, since removing a shared transformation from one arm only can reverse the conclusion;
\item learning rates tuned per arm on held-out seeds, inherited rates checked with the same procedure, and gradient clipping checked at every step so that it does not act on one arm only;
\item checkpoint selection on the reported metric;
\item AUC reported with F1 and calibration, especially under noise;
\item intervals that resample test images as well as seeds, with one correction across a declared family of tests.
\end{itemize}
Comparing only with a linear or multilayer-perceptron head would not have shown where the effect comes from. Reporting F1 under noise without AUC and calibration would have attributed to the circuit a fragility that every head tested shares.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The first four items set the scope of the study, which no further run within this design would change. The others are threats that the checks of Section~\ref{sec:results} reduce but do not remove.

\emph{Simulation and scale.} All circuits were simulated exactly, with 4 to 16 qubits, and are classically simulable by construction. Hardware effects were modelled only as shot noise and depolarising noise, not as gate errors, crosstalk or readout bias. Nothing here can show or rule out a quantum advantage at scales where simulation is infeasible.

\emph{Images, backbone and datasets.} The images were $28\times28$ and upsampled to $224\times224$, far from clinical resolution. There was one backbone, frozen and truncated at one depth, and four datasets from one benchmark family. The nested bootstrap treats the datasets as fixed, so its intervals support inference about these four. The leave-one-out analysis shows that the result depends on BloodMNIST. No data augmentation was used, so that features could be cached. Augmentation might help every arm when labels are scarce, and its effect on the comparison was not measured. The only exception, the pre-plan compression check (Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory}), is reported only as context.

\emph{Effect size.} The primary effect is small, and its interval reaches down to 0.0025 AUC. On BreastMNIST both heads perform modestly. A reader may fairly regard the advantage as practically negligible.

\emph{Validation labels.} Validation sets used up to $2n$ labelled images per class, more than the $n$ used for training. A deployment with $n$ labels in total would have fewer for model selection. The rule is the same for every arm.

\emph{Mechanism.} The analyses locate the advantage in a head trained together with its projection. Comparisons of small and large learners show a pattern consistent with regularisation. The analyses do not establish why the \vqc{} leads the matched head. That evidence is exploratory, and its scarcity-dependent form among classical heads appeared only with the larger head at its selected rate. Separating restriction from other properties of the head, such as the gradients it passes to the projection, needs experiments that were not run.

\emph{Learning rates.} Each arm received one rate, chosen by mean validation AUC pooled over $n\in\{5,20,100\}$. The restriction check shows that such a choice can change the shape of a comparison over $n$. Inherited rates were checked, and the two comparisons they affected were repeated at the selected rates. The rates at $d=16$ were not checked. The frozen-projection follow-up used the rates selected with a learned projection. Its original run, at a different rate, gave the same sign without reaching significance.

\emph{Input bound.} The checks without the bound covered the \vqc{}, the matched head and the Fourier head, at $d=4$ only and on CPU. The low-rank, identity and re-upload heads were not run without it under the primary protocol. The two \vqc{} variants ran at different tuned rates, and no run at a common rate separates the bound from the rate.

\emph{Budget, clipping and devices.} Neither the 100-epoch cap nor clipping changed a confirmatory verdict. Both changed the re-uploading circuit's comparison with its 2,500-parameter fit, which is therefore reported in several versions (Table~\ref{tab:reupfc}, Appendix~\ref{app:exploratory}). The budget analysis used 10 seeds. Identical code on two devices moved the five-seed full-data estimate by 0.007, almost entirely through the circuit head (exploratory). Estimates from few seeds should therefore be read with care. All CPU comparisons pair arms trained on one processor type, because re-runs on a different processor diverged from the first optimisation step.

\emph{Plan and exploratory analyses.} The primary crossover was noticed before the plan was written. The plan carries 33 dated amendments (numbered 1--32, plus 3a), of which four record changes made before they were written down. The plan is version-controlled, not externally registered. Every analysis outside the 23 confirmatory tests, the two follow-ups and the prespecified F1 sensitivity analysis is exploratory. Some used 5 or 10 seeds, and the head-sensitivity measures describe the heads alone. None of these analyses adds a confirmatory claim.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
A four-qubit variational head on frozen ResNet-18 features outperformed a parameter-matched classical head at five labelled images per class, in a prespecified test corrected across a family of 23. The result survived re-execution of every run. In exploratory checks it also survived the removal of gradient clipping, a tenfold training budget and the removal of the input bound from both heads. It was small, came mainly from one dataset and one bottleneck size, and needed the projection in front of the head to be trained with it. The circuit never significantly outperformed a classical model of the function it computes. In exploratory comparisons, smaller learners led larger ones at the smallest training sizes. This is consistent with a regularisation effect, not with quantum computation. For hybrid quantum classifiers, an observed advantage should therefore be traced through controls of the layers around the circuit, of its function class and of any preprocessing shared across arms before it is attributed to the circuit.

Several directions follow. Training the projection and head together and then retraining one head on a projection learned with the other would test whether the advantage travels with the projection. Learning rates tuned separately for each training size would test whether the scarcity patterns depend on the rate. Larger images, other backbones, fine-tuned encoders and more datasets would test whether the dependence on one dataset is general. Parameter-matched comparisons at other depths and with other ansatzes, specified in advance, would test whether $L=2$ is representative. An exploratory depth sweep suggests that it may not be on every dataset. Gate errors, crosstalk and readout bias, unlike depolarising noise at the end of the circuit, need not preserve the ranking of test images in binary tasks (Appendix~\ref{sec:hardware}). Repeating the analysis on quantum hardware would test the circuit under such noise. Finally, the checklist of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} could be applied to published hybrid medical classifiers.

\emph{Is the four-dimensional bottleneck a severe constraint?} A learned projection to $d=4$ was compared with one to $d=256$, using the identity head, frozen features, its tuned rate, 10 seeds, every $n$ and full data (Table~\ref{tab:premise}). The design and two predictions were written before the run. On BreastMNIST and PneumoniaMNIST the compression cost was not significant at any size. On PathMNIST it fell from 0.023 at $n=5$ to 0.001 with full data. On BloodMNIST it cost 0.018 to 0.055 AUC at every size. The first prediction, a cost below 0.02 at $n=100$ and with full data on every dataset, therefore failed there. The second, no cost on average at $n\le20$, also failed (mean costs $+0.028$, $+0.008$ and $+0.011$). The bottleneck is a material constraint on BloodMNIST, the dataset that carries the primary advantage. Clipping acted in 112 of the 240 runs at $d=256$. Without it both conclusions are unchanged, although PathMNIST at $n=50$ is no longer significant. An earlier compression check, with a trainable backbone stage, augmentation and 3 seeds, had found a mean cost of 0.0018. This check supersedes it.

\emph{Does the projection train differently behind the circuit?} The run in Fig.~\ref{fig:function_class} has two of its four latent coordinates pinned near the $\tanh$ bound. To see whether this is typical, 160 primary runs were retrained (both primary arms, four datasets, $n\in\{5,100\}$, the first 10 confirmatory seeds), and all reproduced their recorded test AUC exactly. A coordinate counted as pinned if at least half of the test latents had $|\tilde z_j|>0.95\,\pi/2$. The definition and two predictions were fixed before the runs. At $n=5$, training the projection through the \vqc{} left about one fewer of the four coordinates active than training it through the matched head, on every dataset (2.7 to 2.9 against 3.8 to 4.0; Table~\ref{tab:e8}). Both predictions failed. Saturation was not similar across arms, and the matched head saturated more, not less, with more data. Both arms used the same learning rate, so the difference comes from the head itself, through its function, the gradient it returns to the projection and its initialisation. Saturation did not track the advantage. Across seeds at $n=5$, the rank correlation between the arms' AUC difference and their difference in active coordinates ranged from $-0.22$ to $+0.30$ over the datasets. PathMNIST, with the most saturation under the \vqc{}, showed no advantage. The projection adapts differently to the two heads, but saturation is not shown to cause the advantage.

\emph{Sensitivity of the heads.} The sensitivity of Section~\ref{sec:noise} was computed for inputs drawn uniformly from $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]^d$ as the largest finite-difference ratio $\|f(\tilde z+\epsilon u)-f(\tilde z)\|/\epsilon$, with $\epsilon=10^{-3}$, over 512 inputs and 32 random unit directions $u$ per input. It was divided by the head's largest absolute output. At initialisation, averaged over 20 parameter draws, the \vqc{} was the more sensitive head. At $d=4$ its sensitivity was 1.55, against 0.58 for the matched head and 0.76 for the low-rank head. At $d=8$ and 16 it was 1.53 and 1.31, against 0.75 and 0.73 for the low-rank head. The trained heads were rebuilt from the re-executed runs, each of which reproduced its stored test probabilities to within $2.4\times10^{-6}$.

\emph{Gradients at initialisation.} Under uniform initialisation of the angles on $[0,2\pi)$, the variance over 200 draws of the gradient of a simple cost (the batch mean of $\langle X_1\rangle$) fell from $5.5\times10^{-3}$ at $d=4$ to $3.9\times10^{-7}$ at $d=16$ with $L=4$. This is the barren-plateau pattern~\cite{mcclean2018}. The study's near-identity initialisation, $\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^2)$, had been predicted to give larger gradients at every $(d,L)$. It gave a smaller variance in eight of nine configurations. The exception was $d=16$, $L=4$ ($4.0\times10^{-6}$). At $L=2$ its variance was 40 times smaller at $d=16$ than at $d=4$ ($4.1\times10^{-6}$ against $1.6\times10^{-4}$). Slower training of the circuit therefore cannot be excluded as a contributor to the null result at $d=16$, which also rests on only 5 seeds.

\subsection{Hardware Feasibility}
\label{sec:hardware}
These runs trained only the \vqc{}, on the two binary datasets at $n\in\{5,100\}$ with 5 seeds, plus a BloodMNIST check at $n=100$. Each trained head was evaluated on up to 500 test images. The runs speak to feasibility, not to comparison. The original runs used the untuned rate of $10^{-3}$. Every cell was repeated at the tuned rate, and in all 21 cases the trained weights were identical to those of the corresponding primary runs. These are therefore the models the comparisons report. The predictions were written before the runs. Estimating each expectation from 1,024 measurement shots kept the cell-mean AUC at 0.988 to 0.9995 of its exact value (predicted: 0.98 to 1.02). Depolarising noise applied once at the end of the circuit ($p$ up to 0.05) left binary AUC unchanged up to floating-point rounding. Such noise scales every expectation by the same factor, so it cannot change the ranking of binary test images. The prediction of exact equality failed only in the last bit, by at most $1.1\times10^{-16}$ in 4 of the 20 binary runs. Multi-class AUC moved only through the softmax (BloodMNIST 0.9162 to 0.9167 at $p=0.05$). Depolarising noise after every layer was simulated by the independent NumPy implementation, which reproduced the PennyLane outputs of every trained model to $6.4\times10^{-7}$. With it, cell-mean binary AUC changed by at most 0.0001 at $p\le0.01$ (predicted: less than 0.005) and by 0.0003 at $p=0.05$. BloodMNIST changed by $+0.0005$ and $+0.0016$. Gate errors, crosstalk and readout bias on real devices were not simulated.
